The problem

AI has an adoption problem, and it is not the technology.

50%of U.S. adults are more concerned than excited about AI
10%are more excited than concerned
37%were more concerned in 2021. The gap is widening.

Pew Research Center, fielded June 2025; adoption trend: U.S. Chamber of Commerce; integration gap: Goldman Sachs 10,000 Small Businesses, 2026

The tools got cheap. The trust did not. Small‑business AI use has more than doubled since 2023, yet only about 14% of small businesses have AI wired into how they actually run, and 73% of owners say more training would get them there. Everyone is dabbling; almost nobody has crossed over. That crossing is the market.

The bottleneck on AI adoption is not capability or price. It is trust.

Why Maine

Maine is where the fear runs deepest.

Read those together: half the state is already using a technology most of the state distrusts. That gap between use and trust is the whole market, concentrated in one place.

An economy of owner‑operators, a culture that hates being sold to, and the deepest AI skepticism we can find. If goodwill can be built here, it can be built anywhere.

The proof

One signature from telling AI no.

In April 2026 Maine’s Legislature passed, with bipartisan support, what would have been the nation’s first moratorium on large AI data centers. The governor vetoed it over an exemption for a single project, even while calling a moratorium “appropriate,” and stood up an advisory council instead. No other state has come closer to putting its distrust of AI into law.

That one project is the whole story in miniature: a $550 million data center planned for the floor of a paper mill in Jay that went dark in 2023, ending 130 years of papermaking in a town built by the great 1898 mill consolidation. Maine has watched a new economy move into its buildings and set the terms before. The skepticism is not ignorance. It is memory.

Add the laws already signed here against deceptive AI‑generated images and undisclosed chatbots, and nearly two in three Mainers opposing a data center in their own town, and Maine is the closest thing America has to a controlled experiment in AI skepticism.

Maine Public, April 2026; Press Herald, March 2023; Maine House Democrats; UNH Pine Tree State Poll, June 2026

You do not win a room like this with a keynote or an ad budget. You win it one neighbor at a time.

The thesis

Goodwill is the moat.

Everyone is racing to build better models. Nobody is building the rooms where ordinary business owners learn to trust them. Trust does not come from content, ads, or certifications. It comes from watching a neighbor demo what AI did for their shop, then trying it yourself with someone beside you.

Scarce

Rooms where trust forms cannot be spun up with capital alone. They compound slowly and defend themselves.

Proven pattern

Homebrew, 1975: hobbyists in a garage turned a scary chip into an industry. Small rooms are where fear goes to die.

The AI deployment market is priced for enterprises. Main Street is priced out of it. The studio comes in underneath: fixed‑fee playbook installs instead of hourly custom builds, delivered by forward deployed engineers we train instead of hire.

Train the crew

The 301 kitchen is the talent pipeline: operators who already run their own back office on agents, trained into deployment engineers at strong Maine salaries, a fraction of coastal comp. The research favors them: AI’s measured gains concentrate among the less experienced (43% vs 17% in a Harvard field trial of 758 consultants), while a 2025 randomized trial found veteran engineers slower with AI and sure they were faster. Our crew has trade judgment for the review and no habits to unlearn.

The install pays back

A playbook install is configuration and training, not custom development, so it prices in the $5–25K band and under every custom quote. A handful of installs covers an engineer’s fully loaded year; the rest is margin and book‑building.

The subscription builds ARR

After the install, the studio stays on for the review layer: monitoring, updates, new playbooks. Priced under the market retainer, it recurs and compounds; fifty businesses on subscription is real ARR carried by a small crew.

Why not Amazon’s engineers?

Hyperscalers and the Big 4 field deployment engineers against six‑ and seven‑figure enterprise contracts. A $15K install in a town of eight thousand never pencils against a coastal cost structure. They are not late to this market; they are structurally absent from it.

Why ours win it

A crew we trained instead of hired, playbooks already written, and rooms that already trust us make the small deal profitable. The moat is not the technology. It is a cost structure and a relationship nobody with a bigger logo will ever build here.

Installs pay for the crew. Subscriptions are the company.

Rural Maine does not lack workers or work ethic; it lacks good year‑round jobs. Outside the Portland metro the rural west and northeast have seen almost no economic gains since 2001 (Maine Center for Economic Policy, 2025), so Mainers run a portfolio of part‑time and seasonal work instead of one corporate job. That is the recruiting pool.

Zero‑friction recruiting

A corporate employer must convince someone to quit a job. We just add one more, better‑paying line to a stack Mainers already run. FDE work starts part‑time: a few installs a month alongside the day work.

No bidding war

The employers who pay $350K for deployment engineers have no office here, and no plans for one. Top‑of‑Maine pay, a third of coastal cost, and people who stack jobs locally precisely because they do not want to leave.

Same market, both sides

Sixty‑seven workers per hundred openings means every small business is short‑handed. The labor market that supplies our trainees is the same one that makes every SMB a customer for an install.

The gig stack is the on‑ramp. We add the best‑paying line to it.

Growth

Growth costs a weekend and a tank of gas.

The club grows in two modes. Broad: a founder seeds a new town in one weekend with a borrowed room and the 101 kit, and before leaving, the room picks its own local host. Deep: a 201 build cohort opens only where repeat attendance and member pull prove the demand. No staff, no leases, no franchise.

Capital‑light by design

A new chapter costs travel, coffee, and a printed kit. The local host runs it from there, in a borrowed room, on their own name.

Institutions carry it

Chapters live where their towns already gather: libraries, adult ed programs, coworking spaces. Rooms funders already trust, and several bring their own grant programs with them.

Depth only on demand

A town gets its build cohort when the signal shows: repeat faces, and a member asking how to make an agent run every week. Growth never outruns trust.

Broad where nobody else shows up, deep where the room asks. It spreads at the speed of word of mouth, and word of mouth is free.
