Article I · The belief

AI is the great equalizer.

The greatest way to redistribute wealth is to increase participation in capitalism. Private equity does not buy better plumbers: same trucks, same wrenches. It buys the leverage layer, and AI now hands that layer to anyone.

  • Dispatch and scheduling: an agent’s job now.
  • Quoting and invoicing: an agent’s job now.
  • Procurement pricing: an agent’s job now.
  • Marketing: an agent’s job now.
  • Bookkeeping and collections: an agent’s job now.
  • The weekly review of the numbers: still the owner.

The plumbing was never the disadvantage. The back office was.

Article II · The precedent

The mills consolidated. The woods never did.

In 1898 seventeen mills merged into International Paper, then the largest paper company on earth. One of them was Hugh Chisholm’s Otis Falls mill in Jay, at the time the largest newsprint mill in the world. The men who cut the wood stayed independent for the next hundred years: their own trucks, their own skidders, their own risk. For a century that stubbornness cost them: all of the risk, none of the scale, squeezed by mills that set the terms. Remember Jay.

1975

A mail‑order computer kit scared and confused people. Hobbyists in a garage passed the schematics around and figured out what it was for. Two of them demoed a little machine that became Apple.

Today

The labs spent billions on models, then rented them out for $20 a month. Small rooms are figuring out what they are for. This club is one of them.

Forest History Society: International Paper, incorporated January 31, 1898, from 17 northeastern mills; Maine Memory Network: Jay & Livermore Falls, pioneers in paper making

AI is the tool for all of us stubborn sons o’ bitches who don’t want to consolidate. For the first time, refusing doesn’t mean getting squeezed.

Article III · The ground

Prove it in the hardest room.

2 in 3would oppose an AI data center in their own town
51%already use AI regularly anyway

UNH Survey Center, Pine Tree State Poll, June 2026; Maine Public, April 2026; Press Herald, March 2023

Maine’s Legislature passed what would have been the nation’s first moratorium on large AI data centers; the governor vetoed it over a single project’s exemption while calling a moratorium “appropriate.” And here is Jay again: that project is a $550 million data center planned for the mill floor that went dark in 2023, ending 130 years of papermaking in the town Chisholm carried into the 1898 merger. The consolidated economy’s mills are becoming the AI economy’s mills. Same floors, new owners. Article I is the answer: this time the leverage rents for $20 a month, and the woods can keep it. (Watch‑it: never say the ban is law. It passed the Legislature and died by veto.)

If goodwill can be built here, it can be built anywhere.

Article IV · The method

Manage an agent like a person.

Brief

Hand it context and a goal, the way you’d brief a new hire on their first job.

Delegate

Let it do the work. Your hours go where judgment lives.

Review

Check the work like a manager, not a mechanic. Approve, correct, re‑brief.

No CS degree required. If managing AI is just management, every operator in the room already has the core skill.

Article VI · The classes

One hour in. A ladder up.

101 is not a meeting, it is a one‑hour introductory class built on the six problems. Small room, no lecture, everyone leaves with one of them handled.

0:00The six problems, shown live. The thing you keep putting off, the customers, the marketing, the paperwork, the fine print, the evenings.
0:15Pick yours. Everyone names the one eating their week.
0:20Do it, together. Your real task, with help sitting beside you.
0:55The close. Everyone answers: what would you actually try?

201 · The build class

For members who have done the six things and want agents doing whole jobs. A cohort of five to seven meets over several weeks: mostly operators, a builder or two, one voice who has scaled something. Each person builds an agentic workflow for their own business, gives it a harness so it is safe to trust, and puts it in a GitHub repo so nothing gets lost. You graduate when an agent runs one real job for you every week.

301 · The lunch counter

Graduates wire agents into the whole back office and review the work like managers. This is where playbooks get written.

Nobody signs up for a semester. One hour starts it; the ladder does the rest.

Article VII · The wall

Two entities, one wall.

The club: free, forever

No tiers, no tickets, no gate. Sponsorship covers venue and food: cost recovery, never profit. Demo slots are earned, never sold.

The studio: all revenue

Consulting scoped by the studio and delivered at scale. Workshops that companies pay for and members never do. Playbook exports, free for members, sold outside the region. Small standard equity, always offered, never a condition.

What’s a playbook?

The schematic for a business. When a working group solves one member’s real problem, the solution gets written down: the agent briefs, the workflows, the review checklists, the this‑fails‑when knowledge. Members get every playbook free. Sold nationally, it is the product.

Monetization happens around the periphery. Commerce grows out of community, not the reverse.

Article IX · The map

Learn. Build. Fund.

Learn

Operators learn to run agents the way they already run crews. The club gives out the recipes, free.

Build

Some members turn that leverage into small regional businesses, capital‑efficient from day one.

Fund

Capital sized to fit: a member syndicate, revenue‑based financing, in time a small fund. Exported playbooks are the venture‑scale story.

What we count: seats and repeats, tasks finished in the room, members still using AI weekly a month later, climbs from 101 to 201 to 301, playbooks shipped, towns with a standing class and a local host.

Capital starts reaching Main Street instead of stopping at Portland. The club is the top of the funnel.

Article X · The circuit

Seed the room, then hand over the keys.

How the club spreads: a founder drives to the next town, runs the first 101 in a borrowed room, buys the coffee, and stays the weekend. Office hours Saturday, follow‑ups Sunday. Before leaving, the room picks its own host.

Seed

One weekend, one town. Run the 101 kit, collect the what‑would‑you‑try answers, and watch for the natural host: the person answering their neighbors’ questions before you can get there.

Hand over

The host gets the kit, the deck links, and the coffee budget. Their name goes on the next class, not ours.

Return once

Come back as a guest for class two, then get out of the way. A chapter that still needs its founder is not a chapter yet.

The van, the miles, and the coffee are business expenses. The goodwill is the return.

Article XI · Broad, then deep

Go broad on classes. Go deep on demand.

Growth runs in two modes. Broad: ride the circuit and put free 101s in towns nothing else serves. Deep: stand up a 201 cohort only where the room asks for it. The circuit is the vehicle. This is the map and the timing.

Where we go broad

Towns with no tech meetup within thirty minutes and a room that can hold a class: a library, an adult ed program, a grange hall. The venue is also the funding. A library partner unlocks King money, adult ed unlocks Davis. Every chapter sits inside an institution its town already trusts.

When we go deep

A town earns its 201 when three 101s have run, faces repeat, and somebody comes back saying an agent did the job and asking how to make it run every week. Deepen earlier and the cohort stalls. Wait for the signal and it fills itself.

Who carries it

Not the first hand up. The host worth handing the kit to is the converted skeptic: came in arms crossed, got one real thing off their plate, told three people at the diner. Peer proof is the engine. The room picks; we never appoint.

Year one, broad means three towns done well: a standing class and a local host in each. Not ten towns visited once.

Missionaries preach. Hosts pour the coffee.

Article XII · The crew

Train the engineers. Sell the install. Keep the subscription.

The market pays forward deployed engineers $200K to $500K on the coasts, and charges small businesses $125 to $250 an hour to borrow them. We do neither. The 301 kitchen is the hiring pool: operators who already run their own back office on agents, trained into the studio’s deployment crew at strong Maine salaries. And Maine keeps the pool full: we led the nation in multiple jobholding as recently as 2022 (8.1%, BLS) because rural Maine lacks good year‑round jobs, not workers. FDE work joins the stack part‑time; nobody has to quit anything, and no coastal employer is here to bid them away.

The pipeline

Club ladder, then a short bridge: deployment discipline, the harness patterns, the review checklists. The club is three funnels in one: goodwill, deal flow, and now talent. The hobbyist is the better hire: trade judgment for the review, no old workflow to defend, humble enough to let the model work. Offers happen after class, never from the podium.

The payback

Installs are fixed‑fee, $5–25K, configuration not custom work. Eight to fifteen installs cover an engineer’s fully loaded year, inside one engineer’s annual capacity once the playbooks are standard.

The ARR

Every install converts to a subscription: the review layer, priced under the $2,800‑a‑month market median. Fifty businesses at a thousand a month is $600K recurring, carried by a small crew.

The competition answer: hyperscalers field their engineers against six‑ and seven‑figure contracts, so a $15K install in a small town never pencils for them. We are not underpricing Amazon; we are serving a market their cost structure cannot enter. The humility data: a 2025 randomized trial found veteran engineers 19% slower with AI while believing they were 20% faster, and Harvard’s BCG field trial found the biggest gains among the less experienced, 43% against 17%. The liability is not knowledge; it is the override instinct. We feed our own ecosystem: the club mints the talent, the studio hires it, studio revenue funds the classes that mint more. Silicon Valley hires the crew at $400K. We graduate ours.
